# ADR-0002: Build on 1979 to 1985 galvanised G-body donors

Date: 31 July 2026. Status: accepted.

## Context

The segment benchmark, Singer, builds on the 964. Theon Design builds on the 964.
The two Australian incumbents, Wiedergeboren and Zeigler/Bailey, both chose the
G-body. The donor choice drives cost, supply and the durability story.

## Decision

1979 to 1985 911 SC and early 3.2 Carrera. Original galvanised shell and VIN
retained and repaired, never replaced.

## Rationale

- **Supply.** Roughly 74,000 3.2 Carreras and 60,000 SCs built, against about
  35,000 964s. Wiedergeboren's founder said it plainly: "964s are hard to find.
  There's a lot more chassis in the 3.2s."
- **Cost.** The Australian G-body index averages about A$114,000 against about
  A$175,875 for a 964, and the twelve-month trends diverge: G-body down about 19%,
  964 up about 37%. Buying into the falling market with deeper supply.
- **Durability.** The galvanised steel unibody is the single strongest argument for
  a car meant to be driven hard in heat, dust and coarse-chip roads. It is also the
  foundation of the brand position.
- **Export.** Every car in the window is over 25 years old, so the United States
  exemption covers the finished car without further argument.
- **Gearbox.** Staying pre-1984 keeps the 915 five-speed and the classic
  silhouette, which is the period-correct look the positioning depends on.

## Consequences

The G-body's known weaknesses become the engineering story rather than a
liability: the pre-1984 non-pressure-fed timing-chain tensioner (replaced with the
factory pressure-fed design), the CIS airbox backfire risk (pop-off valve), the
rubber-centred clutch (replaced with sprung), Dilavar head-stud fatigue, notchy
915 synchros, ageing oil lines.

Retaining the tub is an absolute operating rule, not a preference: replacing it
risks Individually Constructed Vehicle reclassification and a materially heavier
compliance path. See `docs/03-regulatory-and-tax.md` section 2.
