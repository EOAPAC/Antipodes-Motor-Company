## 2. Executive Summary / TL;DR

- **Build it, and call it Antipode.** Launch an Australian ultra-luxury Porsche 911 restomod house named **Antipode Motor Co.**, building bespoke restomods on plentiful, galvanised 1979–1985 G-body donors (911 SC and early 3.2 Carrera) out of Luigi Pacelli's Artarmon workshop, at a launch commission price of **A$975,000 plus a donor car** — deliberately positioned between Theon Design (~A$860,000 equivalent, ex-donor) and the local Wiedergeboren (A$1.265m, ex-donor).
- **The edge is real and hard to copy.** The founders already own an accredited master-technician workshop (removing the biggest capital and hiring barrier), a guaranteed-editorial PR engine (Baden Bower), an awards-credibility platform (Global Recognition Awards) and low-cost, high-quality offshore labour (BruntWork). No local rival combines a Sydney master-tech workshop with a global demand-generation machine.
- **The gaps are equally real.** European Galleria is a mechanical service and engine-rebuild workshop, not a paint, panel, carbon and trim atelier, and there is no engineering signatory yet. Those capabilities, plus a NSW VSCCS certifier on retainer, an ATO private ruling on Luxury Car Tax, and trademark clearance for the name, must be secured before the first car is sold.
- **The economics work at tiny volume.** Per-car gross profit is ~A$340,000 on ~35% gross margin (ASSUMPTIONS, §10.1). Because the workshop, its fixed costs and its service base-load already exist, the venture is profitable from the first two cars — the structural advantage over any greenfield start-up, which would need ~A$4.2m of capital and would not break even until ~5 cars per year.
- **Growth is tiered, not linear.** A three-tier product ladder — Foundation (A$975k), Grande Traversée (~A$1.15m) and Export/Signature (A$1.6m+) — lifts blended price toward A$1.15m by Year 5 while volume stays at six cars. Export (US 25-year exemption, LHD conversion, GCC thermal spec) is the highest-leverage margin lever, worth ~A$90k incremental margin per car versus domestic.
- **Capital need is modest.** Because Artarmon exists, only incremental fit-out is required: **A$650k–900k** (ASSUMPTION) covering booth/panel capability options, engine-cell upgrades, certification, brand assets and working capital — versus A$4.2m for a greenfield facility.
- **The ask of the founders:** sign the JV term sheet, clear the name, engage the certifier, lodge the ATO ruling, close the paint/trim/design gaps, and pre-sell one to two foundation commissions — all inside 90 days (§12).

## 3. Market & Competitive Landscape

### 3.1 Global market

The global restomod and classic-restoration services market was valued at US$7.4 billion in 2025, per Future Market Insights' May 2026 analysis, projected to reach US$7.9 billion in 2026 and US$14.4 billion by 2036 (6.2% CAGR). The sector sits at the intersection of three durable trends: the financialisation of collectible cars as an alternative asset class; a generational preference among younger UHNW collectors for usable, analog driving experiences over static concours assets; and finite air-cooled donor supply. Singer's trajectory (commissions above US$1m, waitlists in years) defines the category ceiling; Gunther Werks, Theon, Tuthill and Kalmar confirm the demand is category-wide, not Singer-specific. Credible tier-one entry pricing now clusters between US$600k and US$2m. Even Porsche AG has pivoted publicly to "value over volume."

The relevant demand, though, is not the global figure; it is the count of people who can spend a million dollars on a discretionary object — and, for an Australian house, the count who can be reached credibly.

### 3.2 The Australian buyer pool

LGT Wealth Management's 2025 State of Wealth report (with Investment Trends; 1,191 HNW Australians surveyed, including 168 UHNW) found the Australian HNW population grew 10% to **760,000 people** in the year to June 2025, with total investable assets up 18% to **A$4 trillion**. Within that, **34,500 Australians are ultra-high-net-worth (A$10m+)**, a cohort that grew 19% in the year. Wealth is concentrated in Sydney, Melbourne, Perth and Brisbane, and Porsche Club Australia is among the largest national Porsche clubs per capita globally. Demand signals in this segment are almost entirely **commercial, not digital**: commissions are won through networks, events and referrals — which is precisely why the founders' PR-and-network assets matter more than ad spend.

A domestic buyer pool in the low tens of cars per year is defensible before any export. Export to the United States — where Singer, Gunther Werks and Theon have all proven seven-figure demand, and where the 25-year import exemption covers the entire donor window — is the pressure valve on a thin domestic pool.

Theon's numbers are confirmed by co-founder Adam Hawley (2026): five or six cars a year, all 964-based, production past 20 cars, from £430,000 ex-VAT ex-donor, ~6,000 man-hours over ~18 months. Hawley also notes Porsche built "well over 35,000" 964s — which underlines why the far more plentiful G-body (~74,000 3.2 Carreras plus ~60,000 SCs) is the smarter Australian donor. Even Wiedergeboren concedes "964s are hard to find; there's a lot more chassis in the 3.2s."

**Strategic conclusion.** The domestic field validates both the price band and the G-body thesis, yet leaves clear space: neither local player operates a true atelier model (no staged commissioning journey, no hero product, no registry, no certification moat, no demand-generation engine), and offshore tier-one players cannot economically serve Australian-delivered cars (compliance friction, servicing distance, foreign-climate tuning). Antipode's three-axis position — G-body focus, VSCCS certification as standard, and the founders' PR/workshop asset stack — is contested by none of the five benchmarks.

### 3.4 G-body donor economics

| Donor variant | Avg. Australian market price | Market notes |
|---|---|---|
| 964 (all variants) | ~A$175,875 | Strong premium for manual Carrera 2s and original Turbos |
| 964 Carrera 4 | ~A$142,200 | Accessible base for heavy modification, but supply thin |
| **G-Series (1975–1989)** | **A$70,000–150,000+** | Highly condition- and rust-dependent; requires full bare-metal strip |
| G-body Carrera 3.2 (1984–86, prime target) | A$110,000–150,000 | Galvanised shell; G50 'box on later cars; deepest pool (~74,000 built) |
| G-body SC (1979–83) | A$70,000–120,000 | Most affordable entry; ideal Foundation donor (~60,000 built); local average ~A$114k |

The G-body's galvanised steel unibody is the single most important durability advantage for a car meant to be driven hard in a hot, coarse-road climate. Every dollar not spent on the donor is a dollar available for engineering and craftsmanship — the attributes clients actually pay A$1m+ to receive. Donor price inflation is a risk, structurally defused by the client-funded procurement model (§7.4).

### 3.5 Target client profile

- **Primary:** Australian UHNW male, 45–65, existing multi-car garage, Porsche Club member or adjacent; values provenance and analog engagement; buys to drive, but expects asset-value resilience.
- **Secondary:** Export client (US, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Hong Kong) drawn by AUD arbitrage, the Rugged Purity narrative for desert touring, or access to tier-one craftsmanship without a multi-year Singer wait.
- **Tertiary:** Family offices and luxury-asset funds allocating to passion assets with documented build provenance — the hardbound Build Book functions as an asset dossier.
