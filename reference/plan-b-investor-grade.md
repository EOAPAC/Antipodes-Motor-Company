# The Australian Porsche Restomod Opportunity: An Investor-Grade Business Plan

Prepared for Jeremy Levitt and prospective co-investors. Currency is AUD unless a foreign source is quoted, in which case the conversion uses a working rate of USD 1 = AUD 1.53 and GBP 1 = AUD 2.00 (mid-2026), noted at each point.

## Executive Summary

The recommended entry is NOT a car-building atelier. It is a capital-light demand-generation, donor-sourcing and advisory business that sits above the metal, monetises Jeremy’s genuine edge in PR and lead generation, and reaches first revenue inside 90 to 180 days rather than two to three years. Building cars in Australia, whether for the domestic market or for export, is a low-margin, high-capital, long-payback proposition in a market this thin, and it now has at least two funded local incumbents already claiming the same ground. The smarter play is to own the customer relationship and the deal flow, not the workshop.

The Australian restomod submarket is real but tiny. Two local ateliers have launched at seven-figure prices: Wiedergeboren at $1.265 million plus a G-series donor (10 cars planned), and Zeigler/Bailey with the Z/B 4.4 at $1.6 million excluding donor (about 10 cars a year locally, with an ambition of up to 50 for export and up to 1,000 engines a year). Singer is represented here through Melbourne’s Zagame Automotive since 2021, with commissions on owner-supplied 964s that start around USD 1.5 million (about AUD 2.3 million) plus donor for the newest Carrera Coupe service. Demand signals are commercial rather than digital: Zeigler/Bailey reported eight of its first cars sold and six built or in build; Singer runs multi-year waiting lists globally. No public Australia-specific keyword volume exists, and that absence is itself a finding.

The addressable buyer pool is small but well-capitalised. Australia has roughly 42,789 individuals worth USD 10 million or more (Knight Frank, 2025) and a broader high-net-worth base of about 760,000 people with at least $1 million in investable assets (LGT/Investment Trends State of Wealth, 2025). The realistic annual buyer pool for a $1 million-plus discretionary car commission is in the low tens, not hundreds. That reality caps the domestic-only atelier and argues for a business that can also serve international demand and the much larger restoration and advisory adjacencies.

The recommended model needs roughly $300,000 to $750,000 of working capital rather than the $3 million to $6 million-plus a credible atelier requires, and it can be scaled or exited without stranded plant. The plan below sets out the market, the competitive environment with an updated price ladder, the customer and demand analysis including the keyword framework, the regulatory and IP risk, an operations plan, a financial model with per-car unit economics and a five-year projection, a go-to-market plan, a risk register, and a 90-day action plan.

## Market Analysis

### Global context

The global classic car restoration and restomod services market was valued at about USD 7.4 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 7.9 billion in 2026 and USD 14.4 billion by 2036, a compound annual growth rate of about 6.2 percent, according to Future Market Insights. Restomod builds are forecast to be the largest service type at about 39.5 percent of the market in 2026. This is the credible, restoration-specific figure. Broader “car modification” and “automotive restoration” market reports (Fortune Business Insights, SkyQuest, Ken Research) quote larger numbers and faster growth, but they bundle in mass-market personalisation and aftermarket parts and should be treated as loosely related context, not a measure of the seven-figure air-cooled niche.

The ultra-premium Porsche restomod segment specifically is a cottage industry of a few dozen ateliers worldwide, each building single-digit to low-double-digit cars a year. Singer, the segment benchmark founded by Rob Dickinson in 2009, celebrated its 300th build in early 2024, and its own materials cite more than 300 bespoke collaborations delivered worldwide. The economics are driven by scarcity, brand and waiting lists, not volume.

### Australian demand base

Australia’s wealth base can support the niche but not enlarge it. The relevant figures:

- About 760,000 Australians hold at least $1 million in investable assets, controlling roughly $4 trillion, per the 2025 LGT/Investment Trends State of Wealth report. This is the broad HNW base.
- Capgemini’s narrower definition (investable assets of USD 1 million-plus excluding primary residence, collectibles and consumables) counts about 334,800 Australians, with 30,240 above USD 7.7 million and 2,450 above USD 46 million.
- Knight Frank’s Wealth Report 2025 puts Australia’s USD 10 million-plus population at 42,789.

For a discretionary purchase of $1.3 million to $2.75 million on what is, functionally, a third or fourth toy, the true prospect pool is the UHNW cohort plus the most committed marque enthusiasts within the tier below. A realistic estimate of Australians who would both afford and actually commission a seven-figure restomod in a given year is in the low tens. Two local builders targeting 10 cars each a year, plus Singer, plus international direct sales, is already testing the ceiling of pure domestic demand. This is the single most important market fact in the plan, and it drives the strategic verdict.

### The classic Porsche base and donor supply

Australia has a deep, long-established air-cooled Porsche community. Porsche Club NSW reports over 1,500 members  and Porsche Club Victoria just under 2,000, with several other state clubs; a rough national Porsche club membership across all models is on the order of 5,000 to 7,000. No official count of air-cooled 911s registered in Australia is published, so club membership is the best available proxy and it covers all models, not just air-cooled cars. The air-cooled subset is a fraction of that.

Australian auction and listing data confirm donor cars exist but are neither cheap nor abundant:

- 964 (the classic Singer donor): Australian sold results average about $175,875 with a range from roughly $58,000 (LHD Cabriolet) to $541,500 (Turbo 3.3), with the 12-month trend up about 37 percent  per AutoMarkets. A driver-quality Carrera 2 coupe, the ideal donor, realistically sits at $130,000 to $225,000.
- G-series (1975 to 1989, the Wiedergeboren and Zeigler/Bailey base): the broad Australian “G-body” index averages about $114,000 across 93 recorded results,  with the 12-month trend down about 19 percent. A good 3.2 Carrera coupe is roughly $90,000 to $160,000; a 911 SC roughly $70,000 to $130,000.
- Shannons and Collecting Cars Australian results corroborate this: a 1976 Carrera 3.0 Targa guided at $90,000 to $100,000, a 1988 Carrera 3.2 coupe with G50 at $100,000 to $120,000, and 993 Carrera sold examples in the $77,000 to $130,000-plus band.

Donor scarcity is a genuine constraint on any build model. Wiedergeboren’s founder said the plain truth on the record: “964s are hard to find. There’s a lot more chassis [available] in the 3.2s,” which is precisely why the local builders chose the more plentiful G-series over the 964. Every global atelier competes for the same shrinking pool of clean shells, and Australian-delivered right-hand-drive cars are a small slice of world supply.

## Competitive Environment and Price Ladder

### The Australian field

Wiedergeboren, founded by former Canberra lawyer Jason Wenning with Adelaide Porsche specialist Mark Poole, launched publicly with its first car based on a 1979 911 SC, a 964-sourced 3.8-litre flat-six making 225 to 260kW, and a plan for 10 cars. Pricing has been reported at $1.265 million plus a G-series donor in mainstream coverage, and earlier at $1.15 million plus GST and donor. Melbourne’s Young Timers Garage is the retail partner. It positions itself as a driver’s “hotrod” with comfort rather than a concours object.

Zeigler/Bailey, founded by Porsche collector John Zeigler Jr and mechatronics engineer Greg Bailey in Cremorne, Melbourne, is the more ambitious operation. Its Z/B 4.4 is priced at $1.6 million excluding donor, described as a full re-engineering rather than a restomod: a seam-welded steel tub, bonded carbon panels over a steel frame, a bespoke 4.4-litre billet air-cooled flat-six (300kW/500Nm, more than 1,200 unique engine parts), a CAN-bus solid-state electrical system, and claimed compliance with applicable Australian Design Rules. As of the December 2025 reporting, the company said it had 14 full-time staff, six cars completed or in production, and eight of its first cars sold, with founder ambition of about 10 cars a year locally, up to 50 a year for export, and up to 1,000 engines a year. Build time is quoted at about 12 months.

Singer is present via Zagame Automotive Group (also Ferrari, McLaren, Lamborghini, Rolls-Royce, Aston Martin, Pagani), appointed to Singer’s Global Partner Network for Australia and New Zealand in 2021. Zagame provides brand representation, owner support, service and maintenance; commissions are on owner-supplied 964s, built in California or the UK, with multi-year waits. This is the model worth studying closely because it is exactly the representation-and-support play the recommendation builds on: Zagame captures the customer relationship, service annuity and referral economics without building the cars.

Adjacent Australian specialists matter as partners and competitors for the restoration dollar. Autohaus Hamilton (Chatswood, Sydney, established 1970, Sydney’s longest-established independent Porsche specialist) offers custom builds, restomods, engine and transmission rebuilds and classic restoration. RSR Classic offers Porsche road and race restoration. These firms already have the master technicians, the client trust and the workshop capacity that a new atelier would have to build from scratch, which is a strong argument for partnering rather than competing.

### International entrants and the EV segment

Buyers here can and do commission directly from overseas: Singer, Tuthill (Oxfordshire; the 911K and a 15-car SCRS run), Theon Design (from GBP 430,000, about AUD 860,000, plus donor, shipping and taxes, with only six delivered a year and an 18-month build), Gunther Werks (993-based; the Turbo starts at USD 1.45 million, about AUD 2.22 million, and runs to about USD 1.8 million as specced, across a run of 75 Turbos plus 25 Coupes and Speedsters), Paul Stephens, Kalmar, RUF and Nardone (Porsche 928). Electric restomods are a distinct and growing sub-segment: Everrati (Oxfordshire) builds 964-based EV conversions, with its “Pure” edition producing 440bhp (328kW) on a 52.8kWh pack and its GBP 250,000 (about AUD 500,000) “Signature” edition producing 500bhp, some built in California. Porsche’s own chief executive Michael Leiters ruled out a fully electric 911 at the 2026 annual meeting (“there will be never be a fully electric 911. We stand by that”), which leaves the EV-converted air-cooled niche to third parties. EV conversions face a purist headwind in the air-cooled community, where the flat-six sound is central to value, but they open a different, climate-motivated buyer and sidestep engine-emissions certification.

## Customer and Demand Analysis

### How these cars actually sell

Buyers are acquired through relationships, not funnels: existing collector networks, marque clubs, concours and track events (Goodwood, The Quail, Rennsport), personal introductions, editorial in enthusiast and luxury titles, and waiting-list mechanics that manufacture scarcity. Zagame shows a Singer at its Melbourne showroom and works its existing Ferrari, McLaren and Rolls-Royce client book. Zeigler/Bailey converts through workshop visits and owner involvement (buyers help fit cylinders). Deposits and staged progress payments fund the build and lock the buyer in. This is a trust-and-access business, and it is exactly the kind of high-touch, PR-led, relationship sale where Jeremy’s background is an asset.

### The keyword framework and search demand

No public, absolute Australian search-volume data exists for these terms. Google Trends gives only relative indices, and no keyword-planner or third-party AU volume figures surfaced for “Porsche restomod,” “Singer Porsche Australia,” “964 donor” or “air-cooled 911.” That absence is a finding, not a gap to paper over: this is a market where digital intent is thin and demand is relationship-driven, so a pure SEO or paid-search play would be building on sand. Any keyword work should be treated as a low-cost capture-and-qualify layer, not a primary channel. The framework the baseline proposed remains the right structure to instrument once first-party data can be collected:

- Generic intent: “Porsche restomod Australia,” “air-cooled 911 restomod,” “reimagined Porsche 911.”
- Branded: “Singer Porsche Australia,” “Wiedergeboren Porsche,” “Zeigler Bailey,” “Zagame Singer.”
- Donor intent: “964 donor car,” “911 SC for sale,” “3.2 Carrera for sale,” “air-cooled 911 restoration.”
- Buyer-intent modifiers: “price,” “cost,” “build,” “waiting list,” “commission.”

The practical recommendation is to stand up a lightweight content and capture site, run a small branded and donor-intent campaign, and measure actual Australian query volume and cost-per-qualified-lead directly over the first two quarters. If donor-intent and branded volumes prove too low to sustain a lead business, that is a decision trigger to lean harder on offline networks and editorial. Concretely, target a threshold of at least 30 to 50 qualified enquiries in the first two quarters before committing further marketing spend.

## Regulatory and IP Risk

### Building and registering a re-engineered 911

Vehicle modification and construction is governed federally by Vehicle Standards Bulletin 14 (VSB14) and the National Code of Practice for Light Vehicle Construction and Modification (NCOP), but administered state by state, and the states do not align. A heavily modified 911 that retains its original floor pan and VIN is a “modified production vehicle,” certified by a state-accredited signatory (VASS in Victoria, the Vehicle Safety Compliance Certification Scheme in NSW) against the applicable Australian Design Rules for the vehicle’s date of manufacture. A car built on an all-new tub with the original floor cut out, which is what Zeigler/Bailey does, risks being treated as an Individually Constructed Vehicle (ICV), a much heavier compliance path: an ICV must comply with ADRs applicable to its date of manufacture, cannot use structural components carrying an existing vehicle identifier, gets a new VIN issued by a signatory, and in Victoria an individual cannot obtain more than two ICV VINs in 12 months. This state-by-state fragmentation, and the ambiguity about whether a given build is a modified 911 or a new vehicle, is precisely why Zeigler/Bailey’s co-founder said the ADRs and regulations were harder than the engine. This regulatory burden is a barrier to entry for builders and a strong argument for staying out of the build lane.

Registration, engineering sign-off and emissions/noise testing all add cost and time and vary by state. A national build-and-deliver operation must solve compliance in each state it sells into.

### Import pathways

Donors and complete cars move under two federal pathways. The rolling 25-year rule lets any vehicle 25 years or older be imported with concessional Register of Approved Vehicles entry; a G-series or 964 (all pre-2000) qualifies comfortably. Left-hand-drive cars over 30 years old can be registered and used without conversion in most states, while those 25 to 30 years old are restricted to personal use unless converted to right-hand drive, and rules vary by state. The Specialist and Enthusiast Vehicle Scheme (SEVS) covers newer specialist cars through a Registered Automotive Workshop. Luxury Car Tax is the key tax trap: 33 percent on the GST-inclusive value above $80,567 (2025 to 26; $91,387 for fuel-efficient, $120,000 for zero-emission under the new EU FTA category), but it generally applies only to cars imported or sold within two years of manufacture, so a genuinely old donor or a used car is usually outside LCT. A newly manufactured or newly re-manufactured car sold to a customer could attract LCT on the full value, a material issue for a domestic atelier and another cost the advisory model avoids.

### Trading off the Porsche name

Porsche AG guards its marks aggressively. Porsche Cars North America, with Porsche AG as co-plaintiff, sued Singer, filing on 26 February 2024 in the US District Court for the District of Delaware (Porsche Cars North America, Inc. et al v. Singer Vehicle Design, No. 1:2024cv00253), over the DLS and DLS Turbo, alleging unauthorised use of the 911 name, crest, stylised logo and the trademarked 911 “trade dress,” and breach of a 2012 agreement that had defined what Singer could and could not do and required Porsche-sourced parts. The suit settled on undisclosed terms; Singer continues to build. The governing lesson is in how the survivors navigate it: cars must be described as a “Porsche 911 reimagined by Singer,” never sold as a Porsche; every builder carries an explicit disclaimer of association (Zeigler/Bailey states it “isn’t sponsored, associated, approved, endorsed nor affiliated with Porsche,” and Singer’s materials carry the same). An advisory, brokerage and demand-generation business carries far less trademark exposure than a builder, because it is selling services and introductions, not badged cars, but it must still avoid implying Porsche endorsement in its own branding and naming.

### Consumer law and warranty

Anyone who supplies a vehicle “in trade or commerce” in Australia is bound by the Australian Consumer Law consumer guarantees: acceptable quality, fitness for purpose, and, for a major failure, the consumer’s choice of repair, replacement or refund. These cannot be contracted out of. For a builder selling a $1.5 million bespoke car, that is a serious tail risk: a major defect could trigger a refund or replacement obligation on a hand-built, hard-to-replace car. A broker or adviser who arranges a transaction between a client and a third-party builder carries materially lower guarantee exposure than the manufacturer, though it must be scrupulous about not misrepresenting the goods. This is a further point in favour of the recommended model.

## Operations Plan (Recommended Model)

The recommended business has three revenue lines, launched in sequence, all light on fixed assets.

1. Demand generation and representation. Act as the Australian marketing, PR and client-acquisition engine for one or more builders (ideally an international atelier without strong local presence, or a local builder wanting to reach export and interstate buyers). Deliverables are editorial placement, event presence, a curated client book, qualified introductions and deposit conversion. This is Jeremy’s core competency through Baden Bower and Global Recognition Awards, and it needs a laptop, a brand, a small content budget and a network, not a workshop.
1. Donor sourcing and brokerage. Source, inspect, authenticate and broker air-cooled donor cars (964, 3.2 Carrera, SC, 993) for build clients and collectors, taking a sourcing fee or margin. This requires a trusted inspection partner (an existing specialist such as Autohaus Hamilton or RSR Classic on a fee basis) rather than in-house technicians.
1. Advisory and concierge. Paid advisory to HNW buyers on which builder, spec and donor to choose, plus project oversight and a service/storage concierge, on retainer or success fee.

Staffing at launch is Jeremy plus one experienced client-facing operator and one part-time marketer/content producer, with inspection, legal and engineering work bought in from partners. No master technicians, trimmers or engineers on payroll, which removes the largest fixed cost and the hardest hiring problem in the build lane (a fully-loaded master technician in Australia costs roughly $105,000 to $115,000 a year, and specialist Porsche shop rates run $160 to $250 an hour). Premises can be a serviced office plus access to a partner’s showroom for display, not a leased workshop.

### The lighter-capital alternatives compared

- Donor sourcing/brokerage alone: lowest capital, fastest revenue, but low ceiling and commoditised; best as a component of the recommended model, not standalone.
- Restoration advisory alone: high-margin but thin volume; again a component.
- Lead-generation/marketing alone: plays directly to Jeremy’s strength, but weak defensibility and dependent on the search-demand question, which the data suggests is thin. Bundling it with representation and sourcing is what makes it durable.

## Go-to-Market Plan

1. Anchor a builder relationship. Secure a formal Australian representation or referral agreement with at least one credible builder. The highest-value target is an international atelier that lacks a strong local presence (Theon, Tuthill, Gunther Werks, Everrati for the EV segment) or a local builder seeking interstate and export reach. Zagame-Singer is the proof of concept for the model.
1. Build the client book. Work the existing UHNW and collector networks through Porsche clubs, concours, track days and private events, plus targeted editorial and PR (Jeremy’s core skill), positioning the business as the trusted, marque-agnostic adviser.
1. Instrument the digital layer. Stand up a capture site and a small branded/donor-intent campaign, and measure real Australian query volume and cost-per-qualified-lead over two quarters against the 30-to-50-enquiry threshold before scaling spend.
1. Launch the donor desk. Partner with an established Sydney or Melbourne Porsche specialist for inspection and authentication, and begin sourcing and brokering donors immediately for early cash flow.
1. Layer the annuity. Add service, storage and concierge relationships that keep the business in the owner’s life after the sale.

The overarching benchmark that would change the recommendation: if, and only if, the business demonstrates a repeatable pipeline of ten-plus committed seven-figure buyers a year AND secures an experienced build partner willing to take the manufacturing and ACL risk, then an equity stake in that partner’s atelier becomes worth considering. Building a workshop first, on hope of demand, is the mistake to avoid.
